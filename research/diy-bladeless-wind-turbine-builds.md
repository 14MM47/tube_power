# Bladeless home turbines deliver milliwatts, not watts

A home builder who copies the Vortex Bladeless idea should expect milliwatts, not watts. Every credible hobby, student and small-lab bladeless build found in this research produced between microwatts and about 50 mW. The most credible student figure is **about 1 mW** (Worcester Polytechnic Institute), and **no source documents a home-built bladeless device reaching 1 W**. Physics explains why. A 1 m × 0.2 m mast intercepts only about 15 W of wind at 5 m/s, and the best peer-reviewed model of a Vortex-style mast converts **about 6%** of what it intercepts. A carefully tuned 1–2 m build therefore tops out around 0.5–1 W of mechanical power while it is locked into resonance, and its yearly average is far lower: one 2026 modelling study estimates **about 140 Wh per year** for a tuned small mast, an average of 16 mW. The build information is scattered and uneven. Maker sites hold little beyond a 2020 Hackaday proof of concept and some Windbelt guides. The real engineering is in Vortex's 2010 patent (which works through the tuning maths), its 2014–2017 patents on magnetic tuning and an in-mast generator, its 2018 green paper, student capstone reports, and a large academic literature on vortex-induced vibration (VIV) and galloping harvesters. Other bladeless approaches (Windbelt flutter, triboelectric films, piezo "leaves") are easier to build but produce the same milliwatt-level power. Electrostatic and ducted designs are not practical DIY projects. A bladeless build is best treated as a physics experiment or a power source for a small sensor: a cheap bladed micro-turbine beats every bladeless option by one to three orders of magnitude. One caveat applies throughout. Only one primary source, the EP2602483A1 patent, could be read in full during this research. Nearly every other figure comes from search-result snippets, so check the numbers flagged below as snippet-only, conflicting or unverified against the original before you design around them.

## A 1 m mast intercepts about 15 W and converts about 6% of it

All bladeless devices are bounded by the same equation, P = ½ρAv³. For a mast, though, A is the frontal area (height × diameter), not a swept disc. That is the root of Michael Barnard's critique: oscillators do not sweep an area, so material scales roughly linearly with the wind intercepted ([CleanTechnica](https://cleantechnica.com/2015/05/21/bladeless-wind-turbine-reality-check/)). At sea-level air density, wind carries about **16.5 W/m² at 3 m/s, 77 W/m² at 5 m/s and 612 W/m² at 10 m/s** (my arithmetic). Because power rises with the cube of wind speed, a doubling of wind speed means eight times the power, and that dominates everything below.

The mechanism is set out in the original 2010 Vortex patent, the one source in this research read in full. Vortices shed from the mast at a frequency given by the Strouhal relation, Fv = S·V/h, where h is the mast width. The mast's natural frequency depends on its second moment of area, Young's modulus and mass per unit length. Resonance occurs when the two frequencies match. The patent calls the alternating lift "the usable force" and the drag "non-usable" ([EP2602483A1](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/5d/f7/20/770159c2c1200b/EP2602483A1.pdf)). For a circular cylinder the textbook Strouhal number is about 0.2. A 10 cm mast in a 5 m/s wind therefore sheds vortices at about 10 Hz, and a 20 cm mast in 4 m/s at about 4 Hz. Choosing the diameter and design wind speed fixes the frequency the structure must be tuned to.

The core difficulty is **lock-in**. VIV only produces large motion when the wake frequency synchronises with the body's oscillation ([Williamson & Govardhan, Annu. Rev. Fluid Mech. 2004](https://eprints.iisc.ac.in/17385/)). The usual textbook rules of thumb, which I did not check against a source in this research, are these. For a light, lightly damped body in air, lock-in is centred near a reduced velocity U* = U/(fₙ·D) of about 5 and spans roughly U* = 4–8, which is at most a factor of two in wind speed. Amplitude saturates at about one diameter. Peer-reviewed work confirms the practical consequence: "unlike the galloping phenomenon, the vibration magnitude of VIV does not monotonically increase with the wind speed" ([PMC9693298](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9693298/)). Vortex's own green paper describes a tuned model that "exceeds the lock-in range, and then it stops working suddenly" ([Vortex Green Paper](https://vortexbladeless.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/VortexGreenPaper_en.pdf)). Small tuned research rigs show how narrow the band is in practice. A binary resonance controller widened lock-in only to **1.7–3.2 m/s** ([Applied Sciences 16(1):553, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16010553)), and a magnetorheological-elastomer mast reached **2.0–3.3 m/s** ([ETASR](https://etasr.com/index.php/ETASR/article/download/12314/5476/62799)).

Efficiency has a hard ceiling. Rotors are capped by the Betz limit of 59.3% ([Wikipedia: Betz's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betz%27s_law)). Theory for an elastically mounted rigid cylinder gives an optimum of **η = 0.23** under lock-in, at a frequency ratio of 0.90 ([arXiv 1407.5432](https://arxiv.org/html/1407.5432)). A 2023 study puts the maximum at about 18% ([Ocean Engineering 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0029801823012532)). Michigan's VIVACE converter reached 28% experimentally (37% simulated) with roughness strips, but that was in water at high Reynolds number ([Tethys/PNNL](https://tethys-engineering.pnnl.gov/publications/numerical-simulation-experimental-validation-energy-harvesting-single-cylinder-vivace)). For a Vortex-style mast in air, the University of Glasgow wake-oscillator model of Breen, Mallik and Adhikari found a **peak efficiency of 6%** ([Glasgow Enlighten](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/357207)). Company statements of about 30% capture ([ASME](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/harnessing-the-vortex); [Puerto Cortés study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353011747_Wind_Resource_Assessment_Analysis_of_the_Vortex_Bladeless_Characteristics_in_Puerto_Cortes_Honduras)) conflict with all of this, and Paul Gipe notes that the company has never published its performance measurements ([Wind-Works](https://wind-works.org/oh-no-not-them-again-bladeless-wind-turbines-are-back/)). A second subtlety matters for builders: maximum amplitude is not maximum power. Harvesting efficiency peaks at a **moderate damping ratio of about 0.025–0.05** ([Barrero-Gil, Pindado & Avila 2012](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251574443_Extracting_energy_from_Vortex-Induced_Vibrations_A_parametric_study)). VIVACE's high-Reynolds-number optimum was 0.12 ([VIVACE tests](https://www.academia.edu/24268680/High_damping_high_Reynolds_VIV_tests_for_energy_harnessing_using_the_VIVACE_converter)). These papers define damping differently, so compare them with care.

**Galloping** is the main alternative instability. Square, D-shaped and triangular sections can meet Den Hartog's onset criterion (lift slope plus drag below zero) and gallop ([Barrero-Gil, Alonso & Sanz-Andrés 2010](https://oa.upm.es/7294/2/INVE_MEM_2010_78822.pdf)). A round cylinder, being axisymmetric, cannot. Galloping gives large amplitudes over a wide range of wind speeds ([Zhao & Yang 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2017/3585972)), but it needs a higher cut-in speed ([Applied Energy 2026 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306261926005891)), and its amplitude has no natural upper limit. In one bio-inspired mast study, a plain cylinder still outperformed the alternative section below 5 m/s ([Biomimetics 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/biomimetics10100692)). For low domestic winds, then, the round VIV mast is a reasonable starting point. A galloping section suits windier, more variable sites, provided you build in amplitude stops.

The table puts these ceilings in domestic terms. All figures are my arithmetic from P = ½ρAv³. They are mechanical power before any generator losses, and the mast columns apply only while the mast is locked in.

Per square metre of frontal area, the rotor wins by about a factor of five even at the mast's 6% peak. The gap grows to roughly an order of magnitude once you account for the mast producing almost nothing outside its lock-in band, while a rotor produces power across its whole operating range.

## Measured builds produce between microwatts and 50 milliwatts

Measured results agree with the physics. Robert Murray-Smith's hobby vibrators, covered by Hackaday in 2020, used a stick glued to a loudspeaker voice coil and a window-blind slat moving a magnet past a coil. The article calls their output "tiny" ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2020/06/30/experimenting-with-vibratory-wind-generators)). WPI's undergraduate project built two small VIV harvesters, a bluff body and an airfoil, and tested them in a wind tunnel at 5.4–6.6 m/s. Its electromagnetic transducer "harvested up to **1 milliwatt**" ([WPI MQP](https://digital.wpi.edu/downloads/00000130x?locale=en)). A 3D-printed bladeless mast produced **0.43 mW at 3 m/s** ([Abyssinia J. Eng. & Computing 2024](https://ajol.info/index.php/ajec/article/view/286124)). A review of centimetre-scale VIV harvesters lists maximum outputs of **0.03–5 mW**. Its best entry was a body about 2.5 cm in diameter and 11 cm long, with a cut-in speed of about 2 m/s ([arXiv 2308.04110](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.04110)).

The large numbers do not survive a back-of-envelope check. The PVC claim would mean extracting 4.02 W from the 6.1 W passing through a 0.06 m² cylinder at 5.5 m/s, an efficiency of **about 66%**. That exceeds Betz and is roughly three times the theoretical VIV optimum, so it is almost certainly a measurement or reporting error. The Glasgow 460 W figure is modelled mechanical power from a study that "never wired its mast to a generator". The study also left out turbulence and shifting winds ([Ecoportal 2026](https://www.ecoportal.net/en/swaying-bladeless-wind-mast-roughly-inches/39512/)). At the model's 6% peak efficiency, the 0.8 m × 0.65 m design would need a wind of roughly **29 m/s** to make 460 W (my arithmetic). Either the figure applies to gale-force wind or it uses a different efficiency definition. Either way, it is not a domestic number. The same check applies to the Tacoma's 100 W rating. At 5 m/s, even a 20%-efficient mast would need 6.5 m² of frontal area, which on a 2.75 m mast means a diameter of about 2.4 m. The rating therefore only makes sense at strong winds of around 10 m/s or more (my arithmetic; the Tacoma's diameter was not found). Treat all company ratings as upper bounds, since no power curves have been published ([Wind-Works](https://wind-works.org/oh-no-not-them-again-bladeless-wind-turbines-are-back/)).

The one annual-energy figure found is the most useful number for a home builder. A 2026 coupled electromechanical modelling study estimates **about 140 Wh/yr with wind-speed-dependent tuning, against about 28 Wh/yr untuned** ([Applied Sciences 16(22):11286](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/22/11286)). That is an average of 16 mW versus 3 mW over a year. The fivefold gap shows how much tuning is worth. On that basis, a first build with off-the-shelf coils and no tuning should expect the **1–5 mW** that student builds report. My own estimate for a well-engineered mast 1.5 m tall and 0.15 m in diameter, with adjustable magnetic springs and a properly loaded electromagnetic generator, is as follows. About 8.8 W of wind passes through it at 4 m/s and 17 W at 5 m/s. At the 6% model peak that gives **0.5–1 W of mechanical power while locked in**, and perhaps half that after coil and rectifier losses (an assumption). Averaged over a year, it would yield roughly tens of milliwatts. That can trickle-charge a supercapacitor, blink LEDs or run a low-power sensor node. At 140 Wh/yr it would refill a typical 12–15 Wh phone battery about ten times a year. Hackaday's 2020 post argues that a mechanical micro-generator would need more than 100,000 hours of unattended operation to beat a battery for sensor power ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2020/06/30/experimenting-with-vibratory-wind-generators)). For comparison, the bladed "Chispito" micro-turbine built from a Make: guide gives about **1 W (147 mA at 6.7 V) at about 20 mph** ([Make: Backyard Wind Power](https://makezine.com/projects/backyard-wind-power/)).

## Where the useful information lives

For most DIY topics, maker sites are where to start. For bladeless wind it is the other way round: maker sites are thin, and the substance is in patents, student reports and academic papers. Each source type below is described with what it is good for.

### Maker sites, videos and forums offer demos and warnings, not blueprints

No step-by-step Instructables, Thingiverse, Printables or GitHub build of a VIV wind generator was found. The closest hobby material is the 2020 Hackaday write-up of Murray-Smith's devices, whose commenters noted that **drawing current damps the resonance** ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2020/06/30/experimenting-with-vibratory-wind-generators)). The other is the Hackaday.io "FLAG" flexible-circuit wind generator, a polyester-film electrostatic device entered in the 2021 Hackaday Prize. It is a flutter device rather than a VIV mast ([Hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/181882-flexible-circuit-wind-generator)). The word "bladeless" is badly overloaded in search results. On Instructables it mostly turns up a bladeless *fan* ([Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/How-to-Make-a-Bladeless-Fan-DIY-Homemade-Silent-Fa)). A LEGO "bladeless turbine" turns out to be a Tesla disc turbine ([TechEBlog](https://www.techeblog.com/?p=237591)). Student-project vendors such as Skyfi Labs list "project ideas" that repeat marketing claims like "40% cheaper" without results ([Skyfi Labs](https://www.skyfilabs.com/project-ideas/vortex-type-bladeless-windmill)). A public SimScale CFD model of a Vortex design has no recorded results ([SimScale](https://www.simscale.com/projects/mdiep/vip_vortex_bladeless_design/)). Windbelt guides do exist, covered in the flutter section below.

YouTube offers explainers rather than builds. The Vortex Bladeless channel has a 2020 "How it Works" video ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lirOhO4nKIc); [channel](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCVIBk9xcmPDnsh2hTJpD6pA)). One academic demo shows an arc-shaped magnet pendulum at the base of a mast ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MbSjUa_6FNc)). It matches a 2017 IEEE paper that used **2,200-turn stator coils** and found the arc magnet gave more voltage than a disc ([record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........c9c958c3f97bd2ea3ff3aafbb98e1c3c)). A later review suggests the arc magnet did better simply because it was larger ([MDPI Energies 15(18):6773](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/15/18/6773)). A "magnetic oscillator" demo, a neodymium magnet sliding inside a 400-turn coil in a plastic syringe, shows the linear generator principle a mast base would use ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i0h8sZFFSbI)). Two kinds of video are traps. One is clickbait such as "This $60 Bladeless Device Generates Free Power 24/7" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3wyupCvhPic)). The other is "vortex" free-energy coil videos, which are pseudoscience ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9kqN7ihsOyM)). No video was found that reports load-tested watts at a stated wind speed for a home VIV build.

Forums mostly react to Vortex press coverage, and their value is the scepticism. A 2015 Physics Forums thread questions the narrow Reynolds-number range in which the device must work, and calls the claim that it captures "only 30% less energy" than a bladed turbine implausible. A summary attributes to the thread an estimate of "closer to 10% of a regular turbine on a good day", which is forum opinion seen only in a snippet ([Physics Forums](https://physicsforums.com/threads/new-bladeless-design-for-wind-energy.814428); [page 2](https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/new-bladeless-design-for-wind-energy.814428/page-2)). An All About Circuits thread in which a builder targets **about 200 mW at about 5 mph** is a realistic sizing reference for micro-wind ([AAC](https://forum.allaboutcircuits.com/threads/small-wind-generator-for-couple-hundred-milliwatts.147068/post-1252509)). A Permies thread asks "How much power can it produce?" and no answer was visible ([Permies](https://permies.com/t/58130/bladeless-wind-turbine)). Reddit could not be searched with the tools available. The Guardian reported that one Reddit post on Vortex drew more than 94,000 ratings and the nickname "skybrator" ([Guardian via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/the-guardian-usa/20210317/281947430611337)). The informed critics are Barnard ([CleanTechnica](https://cleantechnica.com/2015/05/21/bladeless-wind-turbine-reality-check/)) and Gipe, whose Wind-Works site keeps a page on questionable turbine concepts ([Wind-Works](https://wind-works.org/wind/small-wind/questionable-concepts/)). No logged VIV builds turned up on FieldLines/otherpower, the forum where hobbyists document axial-flux rotor builds.

No VIV kit is sold, hobby or educational. Marketplace listings for "bladeless" or "Vortex Bladeless 1200W" turbines, such as a 300 W "vertical bladeless" unit at US$420–462, are rotor turbines using the name ([Alibaba](https://www.alibaba.com/showroom/vertical-bladeless-wind-turbine.html); [Alibaba "vortex bladeless"](https://www.alibaba.com/showroom/vortex-bladeless-turbine.html)). The genuinely useful off-the-shelf parts are generic. The SparkFun LTC3588 energy-harvester breakout (**US$41.50**) rectifies and regulates piezo or vibration sources ([SparkFun](https://www.sparkfun.com/sparkfun-energy-harvester-breakout-ltc3588.html)). STEM wind kits from US$24.99 contain small magnet-and-coil generators that can be scavenged ([STEMfinity](https://stemfinity.com/collections/all/products/picosolutions-vertical-wind-turbine-2-0)). The KidWind/Humdinger Windbelt kit is a flutter teaching aid ([Vernier manual](https://www.vernier.com/files/kidwind/humdinger_windbelt_manual.pdf)). For calibration, one linear vibration-generator patent reports a peak of only **9 mW at 6 Hz** ([US 8,957,558](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8957558)). Expect to assemble the device yourself from a light tube mast, a fibreglass or carbon rod, NdFeB magnets, hand-wound coils, a rectifier and a supercapacitor.

### Student projects are the richest hands-on record

University and school projects are the closest thing to documented builds. Most publish photos and architecture but no load-tested power curve. WPI's project report is a free PDF and the best single build document found ([WPI](https://digital.wpi.edu/downloads/00000130x?locale=en)). Texas State University's 2022 senior-design teams built a ground-anchored cylinder with "a series of linear alternators" giving steady DC ([TXST E1.02](https://engineering.txst.edu/senior-design/prior-events/2022-spring/sdd-spring-2022/e1-02.html)). Another team built a rocking design with generation, rectification, storage and USB output stages. It found the coil with more windings more effective ([TXST E1.03 poster](https://docs.gato.txst.edu/252623/E1.03%20Poster.pdf)). A follow-on team noted that its "single bending rod … experiences wear over time" ([TXST E2.02](https://engineering.txst.edu/senior-design/prior-events/2022-fall/sdd-fall-2022/e2-02.html)). Georgia College built a wind tunnel with a 30.3 cm test section to evaluate 3D-printed prototypes ([GCSU 2017](https://kb.gcsu.edu/src/2017/friday/65)). Roswell High School's five-year science-fair project simulated mast shapes in ANSYS Fluent and 3D-printed triangular and tapered prisms, aiming for a turbine "small enough to implement in a residential area" ([Society for Science](https://abstracts.societyforscience.org/Home/PrintPdf/5354)). A Florida project using PVDF piezo strips found a circular sail worked best ([Society for Science](https://abstracts.societyforscience.org/Home/PrintPdf/16889)). Indian engineering-student prototypes mostly use piezo discs and report voltages, not power. Examples include a KSCST project ([KSCST](https://www.kscst.org.in/spp/45_series/SPP45S/02_Exhibition_Projects/206_45S_BE_0352.pdf)) and an IRJET mast that stored charge in a capacitor to light a small bulb ([IRJET](https://issuu.com/irjet/docs/irjet-v8i2285)). A 2024 Egyptian journal paper designs a linear generator specifically for a VIV mast base ([Pharos Eng. Sci. J.](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_379929.html)). A Valencia undergraduate thesis covers a resonant vortex-street generator ([RiuNet](https://riunet.upv.es/handle/10251/143371)). Two patterns stand out. Magnet-and-coil builds give usable current at low voltage. Piezo builds give impressive open-circuit voltages and negligible power.

### Vortex's patents and green paper hold the design maths

The 2010 patent is the best single engineering document available to a builder. It was filed by Deutecno S.L. and Abaccus, with David Jesús Yáñez Villarreal as inventor. It converts motion to electricity with ferroelectric or piezoelectric material such as PZT. It tunes the mast actively, using a control loop that varies the voltage on that material to change the mast's effective Young's modulus. Wind speed comes from an anemometer, or "even better" from the steady drag-induced torsion of the mast. The mast tapers to follow Hellmann's wind-shear law so that vortices shed in sync along its whole length. The worked example is a **4 m mast tuned to 8 Hz, with radius 82.9 mm at 1 m height and 105.3 mm at 4 m**, for a Hellmann exponent of 0.16 and a mean wind of 6.5 m/s at 10 m. It sits on a heavy concrete base with a sealed compartment for the electronics ([EP2602483A1](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/5d/f7/20/770159c2c1200b/EP2602483A1.pdf)). My check: the radius ratio (1.27) closely tracks the wind-shear ratio (4^0.16 = 1.25). With S ≈ 0.2, 8 Hz on those diameters implies lock-in at about 6.6–8.4 m/s. That is above the 4.5–5.6 m/s mean wind the same profile predicts at 1–4 m, which would be consistent with tuning above the mean wind, where the cube law concentrates energy. The patent may also assume a different Strouhal number.

The company's design path matters for a builder. It started with piezo transduction and electronic stiffness control (2010). It then moved to passive permanent-magnet repulsion, which adds stiffness that grows with deflection (2014). Finally it adopted an electromagnetic converter with ring magnets and coils (2016–2017). The later families describe the architecture Vortex actually built: a flexible rod pivot, repelling magnets around the rod, and a coil-and-magnet generator. Reports of the portfolio's size conflict, ranging from five families ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/bladeless-turbine-1.6040555)) through six ([Nord-Lock](https://www.nord-lock.com/learnings/knowledge/2022/bladeless-wind-turbine/)) to 23 documents with 13 granted ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/company/118119-52)). Expiry dates are my estimates from filing dates, not register checks.

The 2018 green paper "VIV resonant wind generators" (company-authored, not peer-reviewed) is the other key read. It models the device as "the mass of a mast … supported by a rod with a certain rigidity", damped by the alternator. It explains magnetic tuning as passively modifying the structure's rigidity to widen lock-in, and it describes strong winds producing a "decoupling" that acts as a passive overspeed limit. It favours a permanent-magnet alternator with an "outer ring fixed to the mast (oscillator) and inner ring fixed to the ground (stator)". It reports a **1 m wind-tunnel model with a maximum mast diameter of 95 mm** on a pultruded carbon-fibre rod, tested at the Universidad Politécnica de Madrid ([Green Paper](https://vortexbladeless.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/VortexGreenPaper_en.pdf); [ResearchGate mirror](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331345449_VIV_resonant_wind_generators)). The snippet gives the rod diameter as "10cm". That cannot be right for a 95 mm mast and is probably an extraction error. A circulating figure of 2.7° maximum oscillation amplitude is unconfirmed. An independent study used scaled models **0.85 m tall, 70 mm in diameter and weighing 1.70 kg**, a useful home-scale reference geometry ([Puerto Cortés study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353011747_Wind_Resource_Assessment_Analysis_of_the_Vortex_Bladeless_Characteristics_in_Puerto_Cortes_Honduras)). Published product figures are about 1 m and 3 W for the Nano, 2.75 m and 100 W for the Tacoma ([Wikiwand](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Vortex_Bladeless)), the Tacoma weighing about 15 kg ([Autonocion](https://www.autonocion.com/us/vortex-bladeless-wind-power/)), and a cut-in of about 3 m/s ([tallbloke quoting Vortex](https://tallbloke.wordpress.com/2021/03/17/startups-promote-wind-power-without-the-blades/)). EU support came through Horizon 2020 project 726776 ([CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/726776)). A 2015 Indiegogo campaign raised **US$77,061 against a US$50,000 goal** ([Indiegogo](https://www.indiegogo.com/pl/projects/vortexbladeless/vortex-bladeless-a-wind-generator-without-blades--3)). No open-source designs, CAD files or dev kits were found.

Several other patents are worth reading. Northeastern University's "Non-rotating wind energy generator" (inventor Mohammad Taslim) is the closest non-Vortex disclosure of a **bluff-body beam driven by both vortex shedding and transverse galloping, with linear alternators** ([US9222465B2](https://insight.rpxcorp.com/patent/US9222465B2); [US9447774](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9447774)). US10982648B2 describes a bladeless turbine with a telescoping mechanism that tunes natural frequency by changing the support rod's effective stiffness. Google Patents lists it as **expired for non-payment of fees** ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10982648)). A US patent on a bladeless turbine with vortex generators also exists ([US 12,698,752](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12698752)). On the flutter side there are the Windbelt patent ([US 8,026,619](https://patents.justia.com/patent/8026619)) and Cornell's wind-vibration piezo-leaf patent ([US 8,884,496](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8884496)).

On legality, the following is general context, not legal advice. Because EP2602483 was withdrawn, its 2012–2013 publication is prior art. EU and UK patent law exempts private, non-commercial use, so a one-off hobby build is low-risk there. The US has no broad equivalent exemption. Selling kits based on the magnetic-tuning or in-mast-generator claims could raise freedom-to-operate questions until the mid-2030s.

## Flutter, film and leaf harvesters are easier to build but no more powerful

The **Windbelt** is the best-documented non-VIV option. It is a tensioned polymer ribbon carrying magnets that flutters in the wind and moves the magnets past coils ([Wikipedia: Windbelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windbelt)). Inventor Shawn Frayne's ~1 m prototypes were reported at **40 mW in a 10 mph wind** and promoted as "10 to 30 times more efficient than the best microturbines" ([cleanergy quoting Popular Mechanics](https://cleanergy.blogspot.com/2008/03/innovative-low-cost-windbelt-could.html)). Wikipedia says tests have refuted that claim, though the snippet does not name the tests. Its microWindbelt figures are 0.2 mW at 3.5 m/s and 5 mW at 7.5 m/s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windbelt)). The device's cross-section is 37 cm² ([Imperial College paper](https://t4awstest.cc.ic.ac.uk/media/imperial-college/faculty-of-engineering/electrical-and-electronic-engineering/public/optical-and-semiconductor-devices/pubs/Design-and-performance.pdf)), so these figures correspond to **about 0.2–0.5% efficiency** (my arithmetic). The microBelt product's 2 mW at 5.5 m/s ([IDTechEx](https://www.offgridenergyindependence.com/articles/2146/wind-energy-harvester-from-humdinger)) also works out at about 0.5%. Two sets of figures conflict. A physicist estimated about 10% efficiency ([Honolulu Advertiser](https://the.honoluluadvertiser.com/article/2008/Dec/07/bz/hawaii812070327.html)), and the ~1 m Windcell was quoted at 1–10 W in that article and 3–5 W elsewhere ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/us/wind-power-energy-green-energy-windmills,news-6192.html)). Neither figure was ever measured independently. The best academic result is an optimised belt that gave **81 mW at 6 m/s** with a belt tension of 0.816 N ([CAS record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........7e8024f083f2d4fb3c9cf088cc770155)). Humdinger never reached the mass market and faded ([Ecoportal](https://www.ecoportal.net/en/energy-hack-makes-wind-bladeless-more-power/16847/)). DIY builds are cheap and quick. The Instructables "Windbelt Redux" gives about 1.5 V AC and 20 mA short-circuit current ([Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Windbelt-Redux--21st-Century-Micro-Power-Generatio)), which is roughly 7 mW into a matched load at best (my estimate). Builders stress that tension must be even across the belt ([blog build](https://buildingstuffinthebasement.blogspot.com/2009/11/diy-windbelt-with-wood-and-duct-tape.html)), and the KidWind manual warns that the classic model "will only work well with a powerful fan" ([Vernier](https://www.vernier.com/files/kidwind/humdinger_windbelt_manual.pdf)).

**Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs)** are the cheapest novel build: a plastic film such as FEP or PTFE fluttering between metal electrodes. One optimised flutter TENG gave 297 V open-circuit but only **0.46 mW at 10 m/s**, with a cut-in of 3.4 m/s ([PMC8066174](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8066174/)). A 3 × 8 × 2 cm Bernoulli-effect device produced **2.5 mW (175 V, 43 µA)** and ran from a 1.6 m/s breeze ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/1a5c3b53c20148a8ae0610eafe0793ec); [TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2020-09-tiny-device-scavenge-energy-breeze.amp)). A 10 × 2 cm film between copper electrodes lit a string of LEDs ([PMC6447574](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6447574/)). Output is pulsed, high-voltage and microamp-level, so it needs rectification and storage, and a multimeter will under-read it. **Piezo leaves** are weaker still. Cornell's best single leaf peaked at about **600 µW** ([PMC5234829](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5234829/)), and an early PVDF-only version made about 100 pW ([Metropolis](https://www.metropolismag.com/technology/lab-report-xx/)). Iowa State's own tree-mimic researchers concluded that piezo leaves did not reach the efficiency needed to compete ([R&D World](https://www.rdworldonline.com/generator-mimics-trees-to-produce-electricity/)). A review puts piezoelectric flow-induced harvesters **below 1% efficiency** ([Applied Energy 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306261926005891)).

The remaining approaches are not DIY options. TU Delft's EWICON electrostatic converter blows charged water droplets against an electric field. It reached a 2013 scale model and has published only "positive efficiency" ([TU Delft](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:e1cfdada-85ea-45c4-b6e4-b798abf5917e); [Mecanoo](https://www.mecanoo.nl/Projects/project/61/Ewicon)). Pumping the water is a major energy cost ([Machine Design](https://www.machinedesign.com/archive/wind-power-without-wind-turbines)), and high voltage combined with water outdoors is a shock hazard. Saphon Energy's Saphonian claimed capture "up to 80%", which would exceed Betz, without independent data ([SciDev.Net](https://www.scidev.net/global/?p=36757); [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/saphonian-bladeless-wind-turbine/24890/)). Katrick's oscillating-aerofoil Wind Panel is rated at 1 kW, but its company-reported test averaged **41.1 W at 10.2 m/s** ([Wind Systems](https://www.windsystemsmag.com/?p=30920); [Current News](https://current-news.co.uk/wind-panel-technology-to-pilot-at-edinburgh-airport)). "Motionless" ducted designs hide a rotor. Sandia found that AeroMINE efficiency falls at smaller scale because internal friction does not scale down ([OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1643486)), and commercial Aeromine units are 5 kW rooftop machines weighing about 1,000 lb ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2024/05/24/bladeless-wind-energy-innovation-aims-to-compete-with-rooftop-solar)). SheerWind's INVELOX filed for bankruptcy in 2017 ([Wind-Works](https://wind-works.org/another-ducted-device-dead-sheerwind-invelox-bankrupt/)). The French "wind tree" is a cluster of small bladed rotors. Its maker NewWind was liquidated in 2017 ([Le Journal des Entreprises](https://www.lejournaldesentreprises.com/article/clap-de-fin-pour-larbre-vent-de-newwind-94670)), and a 4.1 kW, 10 m tree yields about 2,400 kWh/yr ([Energy-XPRT](https://www.energy-xprt.com/products/rotor-magnet-assembly-technology-524579)), a capacity factor of about 7%. Ranked by usable power per hour of hobby effort, the order is: an electromagnetic flutter belt (a few to tens of mW), then a VIV or galloping mast with an electromagnetic generator (similar or better if tuned), then a flutter-film TENG, then piezo leaves.

## Lock-in, damping and fatigue decide whether a build works

### Tune the mast before you wind a coil

Choose the diameter and design wind speed together, using f = 0.2·U/D. Then set the natural frequency through rod stiffness and mast inertia. A stiffer or shorter rod raises it, and a heavier or taller mast lowers it, following the dependence on E, I and mass per unit length set out in the patent ([EP2602483A1](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/5d/f7/20/770159c2c1200b/EP2602483A1.pdf)). As an example, a 1.5 m mast 0.15 m in diameter designed for 4 m/s must be tuned to about 5.3 Hz (my arithmetic). An untuned mast will spend most of the year outside lock-in, which is why the tuned and untuned annual figures differ fivefold (140 vs 28 Wh/yr). The cheapest way to widen the band is Vortex's approach: **repelling magnets around the rod with an adjustable gap**. These raise the apparent stiffness as the mast bends ([Vortex technology page](https://vortexbladeless.com/technology/)). A sliding internal mass, or an adjustable effective rod length, allows seasonal retuning; the *Sci. Rep.* paper reports a **55.7% efficiency gain** from this kind of tuning ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-82385-9)). Smart-material modules shifted resonance by 64–89% ([ETASR](https://etasr.com/index.php/ETASR/article/download/12314/5476/62799); [Applied Sciences 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16010553)). Keep expectations bounded, though. Theory argues that nonlinearity broadens the operating band without raising the power ceiling ([arXiv 1410.1429](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.1429)). Shape changes can also turn VIV into galloping. Two asymmetric splitter plates raised a water-tested harvester's power by up to 471% ([Southampton](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/474694/1/Plate_splitter_Jwang2023.pdf)). V-shaped "swallowtail" attachments cut the galloping cut-in speed by 32% ([NWPU](https://pure.nwpu.edu.cn/en/publications/biomimetic-swallowtail-v-shaped-attachments-for-enhanced-low-spee)). Not every add-on helps: fins at some angles of attack suppressed oscillation instead ([PolyU thesis](https://theses.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/200/13132/3/7584.pdf)).

### Treat the generator as the damper

The generator is also the mast's damper. Standard electromagnetic theory, not checked against a source here, gives an electrical damping of roughly (B·l)²/(R_coil + R_load), so the load sets the damping. Too heavy a load, such as a flat battery wired straight across the coil, stalls the oscillation. Hackaday commenters observed the same effect ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2020/06/30/experimenting-with-vibratory-wind-generators)). Too light a load wastes the motion in structural friction. Aim for the moderate optimum damping described in the first section. Piezo galloping beams hold the best centimetre-scale lab results (about 50 mW), but piezo's sub-1% efficiency rules it out for anything larger, so use electromagnetic transduction for a mast. Coil design matters, with diminishing returns: going from one to two to multiple coil sets gave 0.40, 0.62 and 0.72 mW ([OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40y8R23l)). One square-cylinder prototype harvested much less than its vibration amplitude suggested, because the energy was dissipated in its resistor ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321807100_Investigations_on_a_vortex_induced_vibration_based_energy_harvester)). Mounting the magnets or coils some way up the mast raises their relative velocity ([US11053914B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11053914B2/ru)). Another route is a mechanical rectifier that turns oscillation into one-way rotation to drive an ordinary rotary generator. One 2026 design does this with a cylindrical cam ([Energies 19(4):1090](https://doi.org/10.3390/en19041090)). Electromagnetic sources at roughly 10–100 Hz are low-impedance and high-current ([Tanzawa](https://shizuoka.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/10837/files/181010002.pdf)), so diode drops consume a large share of a low coil EMF. Use Schottky or active rectifiers, then maximum-power-point tracking and a supercapacitor or LiFePO4 buffer ([Sensors 2025 review](https://mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/13/4029)). The LTC3588 is suitable only for piezo-scale sources.

### Design the base for a hundred million cycles

A mast oscillating continuously at 1–10 Hz accumulates **30–300 million cycles a year** (my arithmetic), so the base must be designed against fatigue, not just yield. The Texas State rod wear ([TXST](https://engineering.txst.edu/senior-design/prior-events/2022-fall/sdd-fall-2022/e2-02.html)) is the predictable failure. Impact bump stops reduced bending stress and improved fatigue life in a galloping harvester ([ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014SMaS...23j4012E/abstract)). A U-shaped base has been used to cap galloping amplitude ([Micromachines 14(2):271](https://mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/2/271)), and beam yield stress is treated as a hard limit on harvestable energy ([x-mol](https://www.x-mol.com/paperRedirect/1303165832069877760)). The Glasgow model found that the most powerful designs sacrificed structural safety ([CompositesWorld](https://www.compositesworld.com/news/university-research-studies-potential-of-bladeless-wind-turbines)). Two further precautions are my own inferences, not checked against a source. For storms, plan for the drag bending moment at the base. For a brake, shorting the coil should add enough damping to push the mast out of lock-in. Site matters too. Resonance needs steady wind, which turbulent rooftops lack ([CleanTechnica](https://cleantechnica.com/2015/05/21/bladeless-wind-turbine-reality-check/)). One piezo-galloping paper limits such harvesters to winds of about 6 m/s or less and recommends a propeller above 7 m/s ([Micromachines](https://mdpi.com/2072-666X/14/2/271)). Finally, measure power into a load at a recorded wind speed. Open-circuit voltages, like the student "60–80" figure, say almost nothing about power.

## Conclusion

The research changes what a bladeless build is good for. Home-scale power is not on offer. The real gap is in data: across patents, papers, student reports and forums, **nobody has published a load-tested power-versus-wind curve, or long-term outdoor endurance data, for a 0.5–2 m VIV mast**. Not Vortex, not academia, and not the maker community. A careful home builder with an anemometer, a known resistive load and a logging microcontroller would produce something genuinely new. The engineering levers are also now clear. Tuning is worth a factor of about five in annual energy, electromagnetic transduction is the only route with an efficiency ceiling high enough to reach watts, and base fatigue, not storm loading, is the most likely way the device will fail.

For direction, the evidence favours a hybrid approach over a faithful Vortex copy. Start with a round mast, which is best below 5 m/s. Add adjustable magnetic tuning and an electromagnetic or mechanically rectified generator. Consider a galloping-prone section, with stops, if the site is windier. Patents are not an obstacle for private European builds: the foundational 2010 disclosure is prior art and a key tuning patent has lapsed. Measured against energy per pound spent, though, a bladeless mast will lose to a small bladed turbine or a small solar panel every time. Its realistic payoffs are understanding the physics, quiet operation, and powering a sensor that never needs a battery change.
